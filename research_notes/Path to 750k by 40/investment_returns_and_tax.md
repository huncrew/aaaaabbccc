## Key Question 1: What compound growth rate (nominal, after fees) should a base-case model use for a global index portfolio 2026–2034, and with what 10th/90th percentile range?

### Takeaway
The professional forecasters cluster around 4–7% nominal per year for global equities over the next decade (Vanguard: US 4.2–5.2% in GBP, developed ex-US ~6.5% in GBP; JP Morgan: 7.0% USD for global equities; AQR: 4.2% real for global developed ≈ 6–6.5% nominal at ~2–2.5% inflation). A base case of ~5.5–6% nominal before fees (≈5–5.5% after typical index fund/platform costs) is consistent with all three; nobody publishes a formal 10th/90th band in the sources fetched, but Vanguard's own scenario table gives a 30%-probability downside of −2% to +2% p.a. and a 10% upside of 8–10% p.a. for US equities. No forecast comes close to the 22.3% p.a. that £150k would need to compound to £750k in 8 years with no new money, so contributions dominate the plan.

### Inferences
- A cap-weighted global tracker is ~60%+ US (UBS: US = 62% of world market cap), so blend Vanguard's US 4.2–5.2% GBP with ~6.5% GBP for developed ex-US: roughly 5–5.5% GBP nominal for the whole index. AQR's 4.2% real for global developed equities plus ~2–2.5% UK inflation (OBR/AQR 2.0–2.2%) gives ~6.3–6.7% nominal; JPM's 7.0% is USD and unhedged, so in GBP it depends on the currency path. A base case of 5.5–6.0% nominal before fees, i.e. ~5–5.5% after a typical 0.2–0.5% all-in index cost (fee level is my assumption, not sourced), sits inside every forecaster's range.
- For a 10th/90th-style band, the most defensible sourced proxy is Vanguard's scenario table: a 30% chance of roughly 0% nominal (–2% to +2%) and a 10% chance of 8–10% for US equities over 10 years. Applying ±1 percentage point for the non-US portion, a model band of roughly 0–1% (pessimistic) to 9–10% (optimistic) nominal is reasonable.
- Real-terms planning: with UK CPI at 3.1% and BoE expecting ~4% in early 2027 (Section 2), a 5.5% nominal base case is only ~2–3% real in the near term; AQR's 4.2% real is the long-run anchor.
- Sequence risk over 8 years is material: the US record shows 10-year nominal returns can be negative (1930s, 2000s), and UBS shows equities have lost >70% real on several occasions; a mixed 60:40 has never fallen more than 50% real.

- Cash and gilts currently offer ~5–5.4% nominal, i.e. positive real returns of ~1.5–2% on current CPI, which narrows the equity premium and argues for holding near-term contributions or an emergency buffer in cash/short gilts without a large opportunity cost.

## Key Question 3: The "4% rule" evidence for the UK (Morningstar and other UK safe-withdrawal-rate research)

### Takeaway
Morningstar's latest (2025) base-case safe starting withdrawal rate is 3.9% for a 30-year horizon at 90% success (3.7% in the 2024 study); UK-adjusted work is lower (3.0–3.5% in the 2016 Morningstar UK paper), and a widely cited UK practitioner range is 3.5–4.0%. At £750k this implies £26k–£29k a year of inflation-linked income before tax.

### Cited Findings
- "Morningstar's 2025 research suggests that 3.9% is the highest starting safe withdrawal rate for retirees seeking a consistent level of inflation-adjusted spending from year to year (assuming a 90% probability of having funds remaining at the end of an assumed 30-year retirement period)… up slightly from… 3.7%" (2024) — quoted in [Fidelity Adviser Solutions, "Sustainable withdrawal rates for drawdown clients" (PDF)](https://adviserservices.fidelity.co.uk/media/fnw/guides/sustainable-withdrawal-rates-for-drawdown-clients.pdf); original: [Morningstar, "What's a Safe Retirement Withdrawal Rate for 2026?"](https://www.morningstar.com/retirement/whats-safe-retirement-withdrawal-rate-2026)
- Fidelity's reproduction of the Morningstar table (90% success, 30 years): 100% equities 3.4%; 90% 3.5%; 80% 3.6%; 70% 3.7%; 60% 3.8%; 50% 3.9%; 40% 3.9%; 30% 3.9%. At 25 years: 3.8–4.4%; at 20 years: 4.6–5.3%. The history of the study: 3.3% in 2021, 4% in 2023, 3.9% in 2025 — [Fidelity Adviser Solutions PDF](https://adviserservices.fidelity.co.uk/media/fnw/guides/sustainable-withdrawal-rates-for-drawdown-clients.pdf)
- "Morningstar suggests that 1% in charges and fees should lower the safe withdrawal rate by 0.4%"; figures exclude adviser/platform/fund charges; "Though based on US data, the results shouldn't differ markedly for the UK" — [Fidelity Adviser Solutions PDF](https://adviserservices.fidelity.co.uk/media/fnw/guides/sustainable-withdrawal-rates-for-drawdown-clients.pdf)
- Accepting a 70% probability of success with 70% equities raises the 30-year rate to 4.9%; at 50% success and 50% equities, 5.3% — [Fidelity Adviser Solutions PDF, Figure 2](https://adviserservices.fidelity.co.uk/media/fnw/guides/sustainable-withdrawal-rates-for-drawdown-clients.pdf)
- UK practitioner summary (updated 4 September 2026): Morningstar UK 2024 "State of Retirement Income" 3.7% (60/40, 30 years, 90%); revised to 3.9% in 2025 (30–50% equities); Blanchett, Buffenoir, Kemp and Watt (Morningstar 2016) UK-specific 3.0–3.5%, citing UK long-run real equity returns ~1.3–1.6pp below the US and platform/fund charges of 0.5–1.0% p.a.; recommends "plan for 3.5–4.0% as a starting rate, indexed to inflation, then flex" — [RetirementExpert.co.uk](https://retirementexpert.co.uk/pension-drawdown/safe-withdrawal-rate)
- Bengen's original 4% (1994) was US-derived, 50/50, 30 years, 1926–1976 data — [RetirementExpert.co.uk](https://retirementexpert.co.uk/pension-drawdown/safe-withdrawal-rate)

### Inferences
- £750k × 3.5% = £26,250; × 3.9% = £29,250; × 4.0% = £30,000 gross per year, inflation-linked, 30-year horizon. A withdrawal starting before ~50 implies a 40+ year horizon, for which Morningstar's table gives 3.0–3.3%, i.e. £22.5k–£24.75k on £750k.
- Because Morningstar's figures exclude charges, a UK DIY index investor paying ~0.3% all-in should shade the rate by roughly 0.1pp, an advised client paying 1%+ by ~0.4pp.

### Gaps
- I could not fetch a Morningstar UK primary page for the UK-specific 2024/2025 numbers (the Morningstar UK tax page returned 403 and search budget ran out); the 3.7%/3.9% figures are corroborated by Fidelity's adviser paper quoting Morningstar's US report and by RetirementExpert's secondary citation.

## Key Question 4: UK tax rates and allowances 2026/27, and the holding-company route

### Takeaway
2026/27 headline rules: personal allowance £12,570 and thresholds frozen (now to April 2031); dividend tax rose 2pp to 10.75%/35.75% (39.35% unchanged); CGT 18%/24% with a £3,000 exemption; BADR 18% on a £1m lifetime limit from 6 April 2026; corporation tax 19% to £50k, 25% above £250k with marginal relief between; ISA £20,000; pension annual allowance £60,000. A pure investment holding company is a "close investment-holding company" and pays the 25% main rate on all profits, its gains are chargeable to corporation tax, dividends it receives from UK/qualifying companies are exempt, and it can sell a trading subsidiary tax-free under the substantial shareholding exemption — but an investment company does not qualify for BADR on a later MVL.

- Pension annual allowance "£60,000 this tax year"; tapered where threshold income >£200,000 and adjusted income >£260,000; unused allowance can be carried forward from the previous 3 tax years — [GOV.UK annual allowance](https://www.gov.uk/tax-on-your-private-pension/annual-allowance)
- Employer pension contributions are highlighted as "more tax-efficient than drawing additional dividends personally, especially where immediate personal cash is not needed" — [1st Formations](https://www.1stformations.co.uk/blog/tax-efficient-directors-salary-and-dividends/)

**Holding-company / retained-profits route**
- Substantial shareholding exemption (TCGA 1992 Sch 7AC): "a gain on a disposal by a company of shares… will not normally be a chargeable gain" where the conditions are met; the investing-company trading requirement "was withdrawn by F(2)A 2017 for disposals on or after 1 April 2017" — [HMRC CG53005](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/capital-gains-manual/cg53005). The 10%-for-12-months-in-the-last-6-years test and the investee-must-be-trading condition are described in [Wikipedia: Substantial shareholdings exemption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substantial_shareholdings_exemption) and [Croner-i](https://library.croneri.co.uk/topic/substantial-shareholding-exemption) (secondary; not fetched in full).
- Distributions received by a UK company are exempt from corporation tax under CTA 2009 Part 9A; for a "small company" the conditions are that the payer is UK/qualifying-territory resident and not dual resident, the distribution is not deemed interest, no foreign deduction is allowed, and it is "not paid as part of a tax advantage scheme" — [HMRC INTM652010](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/international-manual/intm652010)
- Close investment-holding company (CTA 2010 s18N): a close company is a CIHC unless throughout the period it exists "wholly or mainly" for carrying on a trade on a commercial basis, letting land commercially to unconnected persons, holding shares in / lending to qualifying (trading) companies, or co-ordinating the administration of two or more qualifying companies — [HMRC CTM60710](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/company-taxation-manual/ctm60710); a CIHC cannot claim the small profits rate or marginal relief — [GOV.UK marginal relief](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/corporation-tax-marginal-relief)
- Members' Voluntary Liquidation (2026/27): distributions taxed as capital gains at 18%/24%, or 18% under BADR on up to £1m lifetime if the company is trading (not an investment company) and the 5%/officer conditions are met for 24 months; the Targeted Anti-Avoidance Rule can recharacterise distributions as income; informal strike-off capital treatment is limited to £25,000; liquidator fees £3,000–£5,000 + VAT. Example: £300,000 retained → £118,050 tax as dividends (39.35%), £71,280 via MVL at 24%, £53,460 via MVL with BADR — [Alto Accounting, updated May 2026](https://www.alto-accounting.com/insights/members-voluntary-liquidation-agency-uk)
- Second MVL example: £400k retained → £72k tax with BADR (18%) versus £143k at the 35.75% higher dividend rate — [SalaryTax.uk, 27 Sept 2026](https://salarytax.uk/uk-tax-on-selling-business-shares-2026-27-guide)
- Announced but not yet in force (for models running to 2034): dividend rate rise (April 2026, done); savings/property income +2pp (April 2027); Making Tax Digital threshold £30,000 (April 2027); salary-sacrifice NIC cap £2,000 (April 2029); threshold freeze to April 2031; IHT agricultural/business relief cap £2.5m per individual (2026/27) — [ATT](https://www.att.org.uk/technical/news/202627-tax-year-updates-housekeeping-individuals)

### Inferences
- A holding company that only holds listed index funds is a CIHC: it pays 25% on interest and on chargeable gains (no annual exemption), while dividends from UK/qualifying-territory companies received via funds are generally exempt. An accumulating equity index fund inside a company therefore defers tax until disposal, with gains then taxed at 25%; distributing bond funds/interest are taxed annually at 25%. (Tax treatment of specific fund types — e.g. bond funds under loan-relationship rules or offshore reporting funds — was not sourced; see Gaps.)
- Holding investments inside the trading company itself risks the "main activities are trading" BADR condition; a separate holding company above the trader (share-for-share exchange) keeps the trader clean and lets a later sale of the trader be SSE-exempt at company level, with the cash then trapped in the holdco until extracted (dividends at 35.75–39.35%, or MVL at 24% because an investment holdco fails BADR's trading test).
- The 26.5% marginal corporation tax rate between £50k and £250k plus 35.75% higher-rate dividend tax means the combined marginal rate on extracting profit above ~£63k of personal income is ~52.8% (0.265 + 0.735 × 0.3575 — my arithmetic); this is why retaining or paying employer pension contributions dominates once the basic rate band is used.

### Gaps
- The 20% "substantial" non-trading activity threshold that HMRC applies for BADR trading-company status (CG64090) was not fetched, so the exact tolerance for investments held inside a trading company is unsourced here.
- Corporation tax treatment of specific investment vehicles held by a company (bond funds under the loan-relationship "fair value" rules, offshore reporting funds, indexation frozen at December 2017) was not sourced — the gov.uk page I tried (corporation-tax-selling-business-assets) returned 404 and the search budget was exhausted.
- The definition of "small company" for the dividend exemption (CTA 2009 s931S) was not extracted; medium/large companies use a different but broadly similar exemption regime.
- Share-for-share exchange (TCGA s135) and HMRC clearance for inserting a holdco were not sourced.
- Whether the Autumn Budget 2026 (expected late 2026) will change CGT/BADR/dividend rates is unknown; the notes above reflect enacted and announced measures as at 28 September 2026.

## Key Question 5: After-tax cash kept from £70k, £100k and £150k of company profit per year (salary + dividends) vs retaining and investing inside the company

### Takeaway
Using the 2026/27 rates cited in Section 4 and the standard sole-director structure (salary £12,570 or £5,000, remainder as dividends), the director keeps roughly £51k of £70k profit (27% total tax), £65k of £100k (35%) and £85k of £150k (43%). Retaining profit costs 19–25% corporation tax up front and defers the personal layer; at £150k profit, retaining and later extracting via an MVL at 18% leaves ~£93.5k versus ~£85k from immediate dividends, whereas at £70k the dividend route is already cheap enough that MVL adds nothing.

### Cited Findings
- Published worked example (£60,000 profit, 2026/27, £5,000 salary): corporation tax £10,825 on £55,000; dividends £44,175; dividend tax £3,881; total tax £14,706; net take-home £45,294; versus £22,893 total tax if all taken as salary — [1st Formations, 15 July 2026](https://www.1stformations.co.uk/blog/tax-efficient-directors-salary-and-dividends/)
- Recommended salary levels 2026/27: £5,000 (no employer NI), £6,708 (LEL, protects State Pension record), or £12,570 (personal allowance/primary threshold; best where Employment Allowance is available); tax-free dividend headroom £13,070 (PA + £500 allowance) — [1st Formations](https://www.1stformations.co.uk/blog/tax-efficient-directors-salary-and-dividends/)
- "For most sole directors in 2026/27, the standard approach remains: Salary up to approximately £12,570… Dividends for remaining income — taxed at 10.75% in the basic rate band" — [FSB director pay guide 26/27](https://www.fsb.org.uk/resources/article/director-salary-and-dividends-26-27-tax-efficient-pay-guide-MCNJNHAH62HRD2BAO4ZNNETOEF3M)
- All rate inputs (income tax bands, dividend rates, NIC, corporation tax, marginal relief) are cited in Section 4.

### Inferences
- The £12,570 salary edges out £5,000 at £70k and £100k profit (the corporation tax saving on the salary and the NIC-free personal allowance outweigh £1,136 of employer NIC), but at £150k the £5,000 salary is marginally better because the personal allowance is being tapered away anyway.
- Immediate extraction is efficient up to about £63k of gross personal income (PA + £500 + basic band at 10.75%); beyond that each extra £1 of company profit yields only ~47p (26.5% marginal corporation tax then 35.75% dividend tax). Retaining above that point and (a) contributing up to £60k a year to a SIPP as an employer contribution, (b) investing inside the company, or (c) building cash for an eventual MVL/sale each avoid the 35.75% layer.
- The MVL route only pays off relative to dividends when the alternative would be higher-rate dividends: at £150k profit it improves the net by ~£8k a year on these numbers; at £70k it is worse than paying basic-rate dividends. BADR on an MVL also requires the company to remain a trading company, so a large investment portfolio inside it can forfeit the 18% rate.

### Gaps
- No published source gives the exact £70k/£100k/£150k figures; the table is my computation from the cited rates and should be checked by an accountant (e.g. Scottish rates, student loan, other income, Employment Allowance where staff are employed, pension contributions all change it).
- The trading-status tolerance for investments inside the trading company (HMRC's 20% "substantial" test) is unsourced here (see Section 4 gaps).

## Key Question 6: How a sale of the business for £750k–£1.2m is taxed in 2026/27 and later (BADR at 18% on the first £1m, CGT above)

### Takeaway
On a £900k share sale in any year from 6 April 2026, with BADR available and negligible base cost, tax is about £161k (18% on £897k after the £3,000 exemption), leaving ~£739k net; at £1.2m the excess over the £1m lifetime limit is taxed at 24%, giving ~£227k tax and ~£973k net. Splitting shares with a spouse before sale can double the BADR-rate gains to £2m.

### Cited Findings
- BADR rate 18% for disposals on or after 6 April 2026; lifetime limit £1m; excess at the individual's normal CGT rate (24% for a higher-rate taxpayer) — [GOV.UK BADR](https://www.gov.uk/business-asset-disposal-relief); [Brodies, 3 March 2026](https://brodies.com/insights/wills-and-estate-planning/business-asset-disposal-relief-badr-what-is-changing-on-6-april-2026/)
- Published example: "£600k gain on company sale, BADR qualifying: CGT 18%: £108k. vs standard 24% CGT: £144k. Saving: £36k." Two-person planning: "1 person, BADR 18% × £1m cap = £180k + £1m at 24% = £240k. Total £420k. Spouse split…: £1m each BADR = £360k total" on a £2m gain — [SalaryTax.uk, 27 Sept 2026](https://salarytax.uk/uk-tax-on-selling-business-shares-2026-27-guide)
- CGT annual exempt amount £3,000; 18% within the basic-rate band, 24% above — [GOV.UK CGT rates](https://www.gov.uk/capital-gains-tax/rates)
- Anti-forestalling: contracts signed before 6 April 2026 but completed after are generally taxed at 18% unless "excluded contracts" — [Brodies](https://brodies.com/insights/wills-and-estate-planning/business-asset-disposal-relief-badr-what-is-changing-on-6-april-2026/); [HMRC CG64174](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/capital-gains-manual/cg64174)
- Alternative structure: a holding company selling the trading subsidiary pays no corporation tax on the gain under SSE (investing company need not be trading since 1 April 2017), leaving the proceeds inside the holdco — [HMRC CG53005](https://www.gov.uk/hmrc-internal-manuals/capital-gains-manual/cg53005)

**My calculations (inference; assumptions: whole proceeds are gain, seller is a higher-rate taxpayer, full £1m BADR limit unused, £3,000 exemption applied, no earn-out/deferred consideration, rates as cited)**

| Sale price | Gain after £3k AEA | Tax at 18% (to £1m) | Tax at 24% (excess) | Total CGT | Net proceeds | Effective rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| £750,000 | £747,000 | £134,460 | £0 | £134,460 | **£615,540** | 17.9% |
| £900,000 | £897,000 | £161,460 | £0 | £161,460 | **£738,540** | 17.9% |
| £1,000,000 | £997,000 | £179,460 | £0 | £179,460 | £820,540 | 17.9% |
| £1,200,000 | £1,197,000 | £180,000 | £47,280 | £227,280 | £972,720 | 18.9% |

### Inferences
- A £900k sale in 2027–2032 nets ~£739k if the 18% BADR rate and £1m limit are unchanged — which alone would very nearly deliver the £750k target regardless of investment returns; combined with £150k already invested it would exceed it.
- If the £1m BADR lifetime limit has been partly used by an earlier MVL or share disposal, the excess is taxed at 24%: every £100k of gain that loses BADR costs an extra £6k.
- Via a holdco/SSE, the £900k arrives untaxed at company level but is then subject to 25% corporation tax on future investment income/gains and to 24% CGT (no BADR, investment company) or 35.75–39.35% dividend tax on the way out; this suits reinvestment inside the company more than personal spending.

### Gaps
- No source states whether the government intends further BADR changes after the 18% step; the notes assume the enacted 2026/27 rules persist to 2032. The Autumn Budget 2026 was not yet published as of 28 September 2026.
- Earn-outs, deferred consideration, loan-note treatment and the interaction with an employee-ownership-trust sale (0% CGT) were outside the sources fetched.
